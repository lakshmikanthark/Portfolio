Practical note / Website planning

Before a website
goes live.

By Lakshmikantha R K · Written 29 October 2026

A business website needs a clear offer, reliable information, a working contact route and someone who can maintain it. Agree those basics before spending the whole project on the opening impression.

This is the checklist I use to frame a website brief. Adapt it to the business; a brochure site and a booking platform have different needs.

01 / Agree the job

One audience.
One useful next step.

Write down who the website is for and what they need to understand before they act. “People who need our services” is too broad to guide the content. A first-time visitor comparing local training programmes has different questions from a returning customer checking a timetable.

Choose the primary action: discuss a project, request information, book through an existing system or find a location. Then check whether the page explains enough for that action to make sense.

A useful brief fits in a few lines: who we help, what we offer, the questions people ask, and the next step we want to support.

02 / Gather the material

Use facts people
can rely on.

Offer and boundaries
List the actual services, what is included, what needs a separate discussion, and any information that must be confirmed before quoting.
Evidence
Collect projects, relevant photographs and examples you have permission to use. Label concepts and demonstrations. Use testimonials only when they come from real people and can be published with permission.
Practical details
Check the public business name, contact address, service area, opening hours if relevant, and the person responsible for keeping them current.
Brand and content
Prepare the logo, usable image files and draft copy. Agree who supplies missing content and who approves it.

03 / Check the journey

Follow the page
all the way through.

  1. Read it on a phone

    Can someone understand the offer, inspect the evidence and reach the main action without awkward sideways scrolling or unreadable type? Try a real device as well as a resized browser.

  2. Test the actual destination

    Open the important project links, download the document and follow the contact route. If a button opens an email draft, the page should say so. If it submits a form, confirm where the message arrives.

  3. Check useful search information

    Give distinct public pages descriptive titles, readable content and normal links. Include the intended canonical URLs in the sitemap and check that public pages have no accidental indexing block.

  4. Try the failure path

    Look at a missing page, an invalid form entry and a contact action without a configured email app. Decide what the visitor should be able to do next.

Technical eligibility does not guarantee that Google will crawl, index or display a page. See Google Search Essentials.

04 / Agree ownership

Know who can
keep it working.

Before handover, identify who controls the domain, hosting, source files and enquiry destination. Keep access in the appropriate accounts and agree how changes are requested.

Record the important public URLs, where the content is edited, how the site is published and how a working version can be restored. Also agree what support is included after launch and what needs a new scope.

If analytics is part of the project, define the questions it should answer and verify data in the receiving account. A tracking script in the source is not proof that measurement works.

05 / A small final check

Can a new visitor
make sense of it?

Ask someone who did not build the site to explain what the business offers and find the main next step. Observe the task before explaining the interface. Record what confused them and fix the important friction.

The useful result is a website that can be understood, contacted and maintained. Keep improving it using actual questions and enquiries after launch.

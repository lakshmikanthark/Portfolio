Practical note / Enquiry journeys

A contact click.
Then what?

By Lakshmikantha R K · Written 29 October 2026

An email draft, a submitted form and a received enquiry are different stages. Make the route clear to visitors and measure the stage that actually happened.

This note uses the contact flow in my portfolio as a concrete example, then explains what to agree for a business website.

01 / Understand the route

What does the
button actually do?

Email link
A mailto link asks the visitor’s device to open an email app, sometimes with a subject or draft text filled in. The visitor still needs to send the email. The result depends on their device and email setup.
Email-draft form
A website can collect the details needed for a draft, then hand that draft to an email app. Filling in the website form does not mean a server received the enquiry.
Server-backed form
A form can send information to a service or backend. Define what a successful submission means, where it is stored or delivered, and how the business sees it. A success message should reflect a verified step in that route.

02 / This portfolio’s flow

Prepare. Review.
Send.

  1. Prepare the enquiry

    The homepage form checks its required fields and prepares an email draft addressed to me. The subject and body include the information the visitor entered.

  2. Review in the email app

    The visitor can review or edit that draft. Opening it does not send it automatically. They choose whether to press Send.

  3. Use the fallback if needed

    The homepage offers Copy enquiry after the fields are valid. A visitor can paste the brief into their preferred email service. The public address is lakshmikanthark20@gmail.com.

  4. Count a received message

    I treat the enquiry as received when the actual message arrives. A click or draft opening is an intent signal, rather than proof of delivery.

The supporting pages provide email links, a visible address and a Copy email control. This portfolio does not provide a website enquiry backend or automatically store the form brief.

03 / Agree the business process

Who receives it?
Who follows up?

Choose the destination before building the form: an inbox, a shared queue or a system the business already uses. Identify the person responsible for checking it and the information they need to take the next step.

Keep the first brief proportionate. A name, reply route and short explanation may be enough to start a discussion. Ask for additional information only when it helps with the actual request.

Agree how to handle an invalid entry, a failed submission and a duplicate message. Test the route from a real phone and verify the receiving destination, rather than stopping at the button’s visual response.

04 / Measure the right stage

Intent is useful.
Receipt is different.

Contact intent
A contact link, draft opening or copy action shows an attempt to start a conversation. Name these events accurately when measurement is enabled.
Received enquiry
A real message or verified submission reached the business. Record its source when known, need and next step in a private ledger.
Qualified opportunity
The request matches the work you offer and has enough detail to agree the next conversation. Qualification is a business decision after receipt.

Keep enquiry text, names, phone numbers and email addresses out of analytics events. Google’s analytics guidance explains the restriction on sending personally identifiable information.

The supporting pages currently load no visitor analytics. The homepage has an optional consent-controlled integration, with no Measurement ID configured in this release.

05 / Choose what fits

Start with the
receiving process.

An email draft can suit a simple portfolio where visitors can use their own email service. A business that needs shared ownership, a submission record or a structured handover may need a server-backed route.

Choose that route from the real operational need. Then explain it clearly, test it and keep the next step manageable for the person who receives the message.
